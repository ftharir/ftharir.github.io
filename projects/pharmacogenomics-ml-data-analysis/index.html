Overview

Each row of the data says what a genetic variant does to a drug, for example increased efficacy or decreased dosage. The task is to predict that effect from the variant, the gene, the drug and the consequence, with twelve classes and heavy imbalance.

The most important result is not the final score but the audit behind it: the first run reported 59.3 %, and fixing four sources of data leakage brought it to an honest 42.2 %. The project documents each fix.

Approach

  1. 1

    Explore

    Class balance (the largest class holds 32 %), duplicates, conflicting and ambiguous labels, which genes and drugs matter.

  2. 2

    Remove exact duplicates

    About 30 % of the 4,698 records were exact duplicates; 3,295 rows remain.

  3. 3

    Encode

    Alleles are parsed; drug, gene and consequence are encoded with frequency and target statistics, computed on the training split only.

  4. 4

    Split and scale

    A 70 / 30 split; the scaler is fitted on the training part only.

  5. 5

    Model

    A linear SVM, then an RBF SVM tuned with GridSearchCV (C = 10), compared with a most-frequent-class baseline.

  6. 6

    Evaluate

    Accuracy, weighted and macro F1, a per-class report and a confusion matrix.

Key decisions

Leakage audit

The first score was inflated by a label-derived feature, statistics computed before the split, a scaler fitted on the test set and duplicate rows. Each was found, fixed and its effect on the score recorded.

Always report a baseline

Predicting the most frequent class gives 34.5 %, so the model's 42.2 % means about 8 points of real signal.

Association analysis

Cramér's V shows where the signal is before any model is trained, which explains what the model can and cannot learn.

What the analysis showed

Drug and gene carry the signal

Cramér's V with the kind of effect: drug 0.51, gene 0.42, variant consequence only 0.08.

Some drugs are almost one-topic

Warfarin and VKORC1 are about dosage in 85 to 90 % of records; duloxetine is 100 % efficacy.

Toxicity is rare

2 % of rows, concentrated in a few drugs and genes such as nevirapine, DPYD and TYMS.

Reports conflict

12.5 % of variant-drug pairs carry more than one effect, often the same effect in opposite directions.

Figures

Limitations, stated openly

  • The kind of effect can be learned from drug and gene; the direction (increased or decreased) is much harder, and rare classes (PD, Other, Toxicity) are almost never predicted.
  • A 42 % accuracy on twelve classes is a modest result; the value of the project is the analysis and the honest evaluation.

What is next

  • Treat the direction and the kind of effect as two separate prediction tasks.
  • Use grouped splits by gene or drug to test generalisation.
  • Try gradient-boosted models and class weighting for the rare classes.
